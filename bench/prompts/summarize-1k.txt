Post-mortem: Checkout latency regression, 14–17 March

On the morning of 14 March, p99 latency on the checkout service rose from its usual 180ms to just over 2.4 seconds. The regression was not caught by our canary deployment because the canary only receives synthetic traffic, and the synthetic checkout flow uses a fixed, small cart. Real customer carts, particularly those originating from the wholesale storefront, routinely contain forty or more line items, and it was precisely these requests that fell off a cliff.

The proximate cause was a change to the pricing engine that replaced a batched database lookup with a per-line-item call. In review, the change looked innocuous: the new function was named `getPriceCached`, and the author reasonably believed the cache would absorb the extra calls. The cache did absorb them — in the development environment, where the price table fits comfortably in the 64MB cache allocation. In production, the price table is eleven gigabytes, the cache hit rate for wholesale SKUs was under nine percent, and each miss cost a 40ms round trip to the pricing database. A forty-item wholesale cart therefore paid roughly 1.5 seconds of sequential lookups on a cold path.

Compounding the problem, the retry middleware treated any response slower than two seconds as a timeout and retried the entire checkout, doubling load on the pricing database. By mid-afternoon the pricing database was at 96% connection pool utilization, which pushed latency up for every service that shares that pool, including inventory and shipping estimation. What began as a slow path for large carts became a partial brownout of the whole order pipeline.

Detection was slower than it should have been. The latency dashboard aggregates checkout p99 across all storefronts, and the wholesale storefront accounts for under six percent of request volume, so the aggregate p99 initially moved only modestly. The first reliable signal was a support ticket from a wholesale customer, filed at 11:20, which reached the on-call engineer at 13:05 after being triaged through two queues. We consider a ninety-minute human-powered detection path unacceptable for a tier-one service.

Remediation came in three stages. First, at 13:40, we rolled back the pricing engine change, which restored per-request latency immediately but left the pricing database connection pool saturated with retry backlog for a further twenty minutes. Second, we disabled the retry middleware's automatic re-submission for checkout requests, since a duplicate checkout retry is never safe to fire blindly at a service that mutates order state; idempotency keys had been on the roadmap for two quarters and this incident promoted them to in-progress. Third, we added a per-storefront latency breakdown to the dashboard and an alert on wholesale p99 specifically, with a threshold of 500ms sustained for five minutes.

The deeper lessons are organizational rather than technical. The canary environment's synthetic traffic has drifted badly from production reality; a cart-size distribution sampled from production would have caught this immediately. Cache-dependent code paths need review annotations stating the expected hit rate and the consequence of a miss, because "it's cached" is doing a lot of unexamined work in our design reviews. And support-ticket-to-pager latency is now a tracked metric with a target of fifteen minutes, because our customers should never be a faster monitoring system than our monitoring system.

Follow-up items are owned and dated: production-sampled canary traffic (platform team, 30 April), idempotency keys for all order mutations (checkout team, 15 May), per-storefront SLOs (SRE, 22 April), and a game-day exercising connection pool exhaustion on shared databases (SRE, 6 June). We will review completion at the May reliability council.
